Why patient leaflets should be blog posts

Many clinics are sold a library of patient leaflets that visitors can only download after giving an email address. It feels like good marketing. In most cases it is the opposite.

By Aidan Lehane19 October 20265 min read
{{thumb:leaflets}}

How gated leaflet libraries work

The model is familiar. A provider offers a set of professionally designed patient leaflets on common conditions: low back pain, ankle sprains, knee pain after running. They sit on your website behind a form. A visitor enters their name and email address and receives a PDF. The clinic gets a lead, and the patient gets a leaflet.

On paper, everyone wins. In practice, the clinic gives away most of the value its clinical knowledge could create.

Problem one: search engines cannot read it

Patients rarely start by visiting a clinic website. They start by searching: "why does my knee hurt when I run", "how long does a sprained ankle take to heal". The pages that answer those questions well are the ones that appear.

Content behind an email form cannot appear in those results, because search engines cannot fill in the form to read it. A clinic can have dozens of carefully written leaflets and get no search visibility from any of them. The same writing, published as open pages, could be bringing patients in every week.

Problem two: it is often not yours

Leaflet libraries are usually licensed to many clinics at once. The same text about plantar fasciitis may sit behind forms on a large number of other clinic websites. Even if it were published openly, identical text spread across many sites gives no clinic an advantage, and says nothing about how your clinic in particular works.

A guide written for your clinic can describe how your team assesses the problem, what a first appointment involves and when someone should come in. That is information a patient cannot get anywhere else.

Problem three: it puts a hurdle in front of the patient

Think about who is reading. Someone in pain, often on a phone, often in the evening, looking for a straight answer. Asking them to hand over an email address before they can read anything is a hurdle at exactly the wrong moment. Some will fill it in. Many will go back to the search results and read a page that simply answers the question.

Then there is the PDF itself. Downloaded files are awkward to read on a phone, and there is usually no booking button inside them. The patient finishes reading and has to find their way back to your site.

Problem four: the email list rarely gets used

The case for gating is that the clinic builds an email list. It is worth asking honestly whether your clinic will ever send regular, useful emails to that list. And a list of people who wanted one leaflet is not the same as a list of people who want to hear from a clinic. An open guide with a clear booking link does more for most clinics than an email address they will never contact.

What an open patient guide looks like

The alternative is to publish each guide as a normal page on your own site, in a clearly organised library. A good guide:

  • Answers the question a patient is actually searching for, in plain language.
  • Explains how your clinic assesses the problem and what a first appointment involves.
  • Says clearly when someone should seek assessment, and when they need urgent care.
  • Names the clinician who reviewed it and shows the date it was last reviewed.
  • Avoids promises about outcomes or claims about treatments.
  • Has a booking button beside the content, not hidden at the end.

This fits closely with Google's published guidance on creating helpful, reliable, people-first content: clear authorship, real expertise and content written to help the reader rather than to capture them.

"But we still want to collect emails"

That is fine. Offer an optional newsletter or a printable version for people who want one. The difference is that the guide itself is open to everyone, and the email address is offered by choice, not demanded as an entry fee.

"Is it safe to publish clinical information openly?"

It is as safe as the review process behind it. Every guide should be checked for accuracy by a qualified clinician before it goes live, reviewed again periodically, and written to encourage proper assessment rather than self-diagnosis. The same standard should apply to a gated leaflet anyway. Publishing it openly just means more people benefit from the work.

Turning existing leaflets into guides

  1. Check who owns the text. If your leaflets are licensed from a provider, you may not have the right to republish them as web pages. Read the licence before copying anything.
  2. Pick the conditions you see most. Start with the problems that make up most of your new patient appointments.
  3. Write fresh, clinic-specific guides. Use your clinicians' knowledge of how they assess and treat these problems, without promising results.
  4. Review, publish and link. Clinical sign-off, then publish on your site and link each guide to the relevant service and booking pages.
  5. Measure. Track which guides lead to bookings, and use that to choose the next ones.

The short version

Your clinical knowledge is one of the most persuasive things your clinic has. Locked in a PDF, it helps a handful of people who were already on your site. Published openly, it helps patients find you in the first place, and gives them a reason to book.
